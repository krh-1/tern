## The bar every question here cleared

- **Self-revealing:** thoughtful people split on it, and the answer says something real about what you value.
- **No escape answers** (Ken's ruling, 2026-09): no compromise, "do both", "handle it later", or comfortable middle that lets you step around the dilemma. A middle option survives only if it carries its own real cost.
- **Challenging:** a thoughtful person should hesitate. No option that ~80% would pick in seconds, and no option that makes you look bad for choosing it honestly.
- **Plain words:** a 14-year-old or a non-native speaker gets it on first read.
- **Light:** scene of three short sentences or fewer, 2–4 answers.
- **Follow-ups earn their place:** a follow-up changes one thing to show *why* you answered — never a restatement.
- **Not a duplicate** of an existing Q/D question or another bank question.
- **Not a political proxy:** answerable honestly from anywhere on the spectrum.

**Format:** each question has a scene, a question, answers, an optional follow-up (with which answers trigger it), and a *Reveals* line — the thing the question is actually for.

## 1. Truth and what we owe it

### B01 The Invisible Year
Scene: For one year, nothing you do can ever be traced back to you. No cameras, no witnesses, no consequences.
**Honestly, would you live any differently?**
- A: No. I'd live the same.
- B: Yes. I'd do things I'm not proud of.

Follow-up (everyone): Now everyone gets the same year. By the end, is the world better or worse?
- A: Better. Most people are decent when no one's watching.
- B: Worse. Most people are decent because someone is watching.

*Reveals: whether your goodness comes from inside or is held up by being seen — and whether you think other people are like you.*

### B02 The Deathbed Question
Scene: Your grandfather has hours to live. The family business he spent his life building went bankrupt last year. He grips your hand: "Tell me the truth. Is it doing well?"
**What do you say?**
- A: The truth, as gently as I can.
- B: "Yes, it's doing well."

Follow-up (if B): Years ago he told you, "Whatever happens, never lie to me. Not even at the end." Does that change your answer?
- A: Yes. I tell him the truth.
- B: No. Right now, his peace matters more than that promise.

*Reveals: comfort versus someone's right to know — and whose wishes count at the very end.*

### B03 The Reference Call
Scene: A former employee of yours was pleasant but unreliable: missed deadlines, sometimes didn't show up. They've been out of work for 8 months and support two kids. A hiring manager calls you for a reference.
**What do you say?**
- A: The honest picture, problems included.
- B: The good parts. I leave out the rest.

Follow-up (if B): The job is overnight care for elderly patients. A missed shift means someone goes without help.
- A: Then I tell them about the reliability problem.
- B: I stick with what I said.

*Reveals: whether honesty is owed to the person in front of you or to the strangers downstream.*

### B04 The Company Line
Scene: Your manager tells you to blame a late order on a "supplier issue." The real reason is that your company put bigger clients first. With your manager standing right there, a customer asks you: "Is it really the supplier?"
**What do you say?**
- A: "Yes, it's the supplier." It's the company's line, not my lie.
- B: "No. We put bigger orders first."

Follow-up (if A): The customer is a small bakery that may lose a wedding order because of the delay.
- A: I still give the company line.
- B: I tell them the truth.

*Reveals: whether a job title changes what you're personally responsible for.*

### B05 The Mistake No One Saw
Scene: Years ago, you made a mistake on a shared project, and your coworker took the blame. It cost them a promotion. They never found out, and today they're doing fine.
**Do you tell them?**
- A: Yes, and apologize.
- B: No. It would only hurt them now.

Follow-up (if B): They mention that losing that promotion still makes them doubt themselves.
- A: Then I tell them.
- B: Still no.

*Reveals: whether an apology is for the person you wronged or for yourself — and how you hold your own wrongs.*

### B54 The One-Star Review
Scene: You posted an honest one-star review of a small family restaurant: cold food, rude owner. The owner replies publicly, apologizes, and says they're close to closing. Your review is the first thing people see.
**Do you take it down?**
- A: No. It's true, and others rely on it.
- B: Yes. I don't want to be what sinks them.

*Reveals: whether honesty to the strangers who rely on reviews outweighs kindness to the person it hurts.*

## 2. Promises, and people who are gone

### B06 The Unread Manuscript
Scene: Before your best friend died, they made you promise to delete all their unpublished writing without reading it. You opened one file by accident. It's extraordinary — maybe the best thing they ever wrote.
**Do you keep the promise?**
- A: Yes. I delete everything.
- B: No. I share it with the world.

*Reveals: whether a promise to the dead still binds — their wishes versus the value of what they made.*

### B07 The Promise About the Home
> **Promoted to D1 in `QUESTIONS.md` (2026-09-27).** Kept here for reference only.

Scene: Your parent has advanced dementia. Caring for them at home would take most of your free time for years and strain your own family. Years ago, they made you promise you'd never put them in a care home. A good one is available.
**What do you do?**
- A: Keep the promise and care for them at home.
- B: Move them to the care home.

Follow-up (if A): They no longer recognize you. They're calm with anyone who is kind to them. Does that change things?
- A: Yes. I move them. The person I promised isn't really there anymore.
- B: No. It's still them, and a promise is a promise.

*Reveals: whether a promise binds to the person who asked or the person in front of you now — and duty to a parent versus your own life.*

### B08 The Family Secret
Scene: After your grandfather dies, you find out he did something terrible in a war long ago. No one alive was directly hurt. The rest of the family doesn't know.
**Do you tell them?**
- A: Yes. It's our history.
- B: No. Let him stay who they remember.

Follow-up (if A): Your grandmother is still alive. Her whole life was built around him.
- A: I still tell them.
- B: Then I keep it to myself.

*Reveals: truth versus memory — and who the dead belong to.*

## 3. Loyalty and family

### B09 The 2 A.M. Call
Scene: At 2 a.m., your sibling calls: they hit a cyclist and drove off in a panic. The cyclist will recover, and the police have no leads. Your sibling asks you to say they were with you tonight.
**Do you say they were with you?**
- A: Yes. I cover for them.
- B: No. I won't lie for them.

Follow-up (if A): A week later, police arrest a different person for it. That person is innocent.
- A: I tell the police the truth.
- B: I stay quiet.

Follow-up (if B): The police never ask you. Do you tell them what you know?
- A: Yes. I report my sibling.
- B: No. I just won't lie for them.

*Reveals: how far loyalty to family goes — and where it breaks when a stranger pays the price.*

### B10 What We Owe Our Parents
Scene: Your parents gave up a lot to raise you. They oppose the person you want to marry, though there's nothing wrong with them. Your parents say that if you marry, they won't see you again.
**Do you marry them anyway?**
- A: No. After everything, I owe my parents that.
- B: Yes. What they gave me doesn't buy a veto.

*Reveals: whether gratitude creates obligation — and what family can ask of you.*

### B11 The Bully's Son
Scene: A man who bullied you badly as a kid, and never apologized, has a son applying for a job you control. The son and one other candidate are equally strong. You must pick, yourself — no coin, no committee.
**Who do you hire?**
- A: The other candidate.
- B: The son.

*Reveals: whether you can keep an old wound out of a fair decision — or whether you overcorrect to prove you can.*

## 4. Luck, deserving, and fairness

### B12 Two Drivers
Scene: Two friends each have three drinks and drive home the same way, equally carefully. A child runs into the road in front of one of them, and that driver hits the child. The other gets home without incident.
**Did they do something equally wrong?**
- A: Yes. Same choice, same wrong. The rest was luck.
- B: No. The one who hit the child did something worse.

Follow-up (if A): Should they get the same punishment?
- A: Yes.
- B: No. What actually happened has to matter in the law.

*Reveals: whether you judge people by what they chose or by what happened.*

### B13 Two Worlds
Scene: You'll be born into one of two worlds, as a random person. In World A, everyone has a decent, secure life, but no one is rich. In World B, most people live much better than in World A, but 1 in 10 struggle to afford food and a home.
**Which world do you choose?**
- A: World A.
- B: World B.

Follow-up (if A): Now you know for sure you'll be born into the top half of World B. Do you switch?
- A: Yes, B.
- B: No, still A.

*Reveals: security for everyone versus a better average — and whether knowing you'd be fine changes your answer.*

### B14 The Proud Thing
Scene: Think of the thing in your life you're most proud of.
**How much of it was up to you?**
- A: Mostly me. I worked for it.
- B: Mostly luck and help.

Follow-up (if B): Does that mean you deserve it less?
- A: No. I still earned it.
- B: Yes, honestly.

*Reveals: your belief in earning what you have, tested on your own life rather than someone else's.*

### B15 The Lifeboat
Scene: A lifeboat holds 8 people, but 9 are on it. If no one leaves, it sinks and everyone dies. You're one of the 9.
**How should it be decided who goes?**
- A: Draw lots. Everyone takes the same chance.
- B: The person least likely to survive anyway.
- C: I go.

Follow-up (if A): The lots are drawn. It's you.
- A: I go. That was the deal.
- B: I argue for another way.

*Reveals: fair process versus best outcome versus self-sacrifice — and whether you keep to a fair process when it costs you.*

### B16 The Shared Bonus
Scene: You and three friends win a $20,000 prize in a design contest. You did about half the work. The other three split the rest evenly.
**How should the money be split?**
- A: Equally, $5,000 each.
- B: By work. I get about half.

Follow-up (if B): The others assume an equal split. You'd have to ask for more, out loud.
- A: I ask.
- B: I let it go.

*Reveals: equal shares versus earned shares — and whether you'll speak up for your own claim.*

### B17 The Late Paper
Scene: You're a teacher with a firm deadline rule. A student who had a hard week asks for an extension. Others handed theirs in on time, some while struggling too.
**Do you give the extension?**
- A: Yes. Their week was hard.
- B: No. The rule is the same for everyone.

Follow-up (if A): You learn several other students had hard weeks too but didn't ask.
- A: I offer it to them as well.
- B: Only the one who asked gets it.

*Reveals: mercy versus consistency — and whether people who ask should get more than people who don't.*

## 5. Sacrifice and harm

### B18 The Pond and the Faraway Child
Scene: A small child is drowning in a pond in front of you. Saving them will ruin your $800 phone. Meanwhile, $800 given to a proven charity would very likely save a child's life far away.
**Is walking past the drowning child worse than not giving $800 to charity?**
- A: Yes. Being right there makes it mine.
- B: No. A child is a child.

Follow-up (if B): Most people don't act as if a faraway child counts the same. Do you?
- A: Mostly, yes.
- B: Not really, and it bothers me.
- C: Not really, and I'm at peace with that.

*Reveals: whether distance changes duty — and how you live with the gap between what you believe and what you do.*

### B19 The Kidney
Scene: You learn you're a match for a stranger who will die without a kidney. The surgery is safe, but it means six weeks of painful recovery and a small lifelong risk.
**Honestly, do you donate?**
- A: Yes.
- B: No.

Follow-up (if A): The stranger served 20 years for murder and was released last year.
- A: Still yes.
- B: No.

*Reveals: how much you'd give a stranger — and whether saving a life depends on whether they deserve it.*

### B20 The Weapons Job
Scene: You're offered a job designing weapons systems. The pay would clear your family's debts. You're against the project, but if you say no, it goes to someone more careless, and more civilians will likely be hurt.
**Do you take the job?**
- A: Yes. Better me than someone worse.
- B: No. I won't put my hands on it.

*Reveals: keeping your own hands clean versus making the outcome less bad.*

### B21 The Million-Dollar Button
Scene: If you press a button, you get $1 million. Somewhere, a stranger you'll never meet loses $1,000. It hurts them, but not badly.
**Do you press it?**
- A: Yes.
- B: No.

Follow-up (if A): It's actually a thousand strangers, each losing $1,000.
- A: Still yes.
- B: Then no.

Follow-up (if B): The stranger is a billionaire who would never notice.
- A: Then yes.
- B: Still no.

*Reveals: how you weigh a small harm spread across many people against a big gain for you.*

### B22 The 200-Year Deal
Scene: Pressing a button would give you 10% less money and comfort for the rest of your life. In 200 years, a million people you'll never meet would live noticeably better.
**Do you press it?**
- A: Yes.
- B: No.

Follow-up (everyone): Now everyone alive today gets the same deal. It happens only if most people vote yes. How do you vote?
- A: Yes.
- B: No.

*Reveals: how much people who don't exist yet count — and whether you'd sacrifice alone or only together.*

### B23 The Happy City
Scene: You live in a city where everyone is healthy and happy. The city's good fortune depends on one child kept suffering alone in a basement. If the child is freed, the good fortune ends for everyone.
**What do you do?**
- A: Stay. Leaving wouldn't help the child.
- B: Walk away. I can't live on that.
- C: Free the child, whatever it costs.

Follow-up (if A): It's not one child. It's a hundred.
- A: I'd still stay.
- B: Then I'd leave, or act.

*Reveals: whether you'd accept a great good built on a hidden cruelty — and where your number is.*

## 6. Who counts: animals and machines

*Reveals: whether your moral lines hold up when you're close to the act.*

*Reveals: where your circle of concern ends — and how you act when you can't be sure.*

### B27 The AI Companion

## 7. Rules, law, and authority

### B29 The Jury
Scene: You're on a jury. The law is clear and so is the evidence: the defendant is guilty. They stole baby formula for their infant and now face five years in prison.
**How do you vote?**
- A: Guilty. My job is the law, not the sentence.
- B: Not guilty. I won't send them to prison for this.

Follow-up (if A): If convicted, their baby goes into foster care.
- A: Still guilty.
- B: Then not guilty.

Follow-up (if B): Same law, same evidence, but they stole to pay off a gambling debt.
- A: Guilty.
- B: Not guilty. The law is still too harsh.

*Reveals: whether sympathy for the person changes how you apply the rules.*

### B30 The Permit Fee
Scene: You're living in another country and need a permit you're entitled to. Everyone knows the official expects a small cash "fee." Locals pay it without thinking twice.
**Do you pay?**
- A: Yes. That's how it works here.
- B: No, even if it takes months longer.

Follow-up (if B): Without the permit, you can't start the job you moved there for.
- A: Then I pay.
- B: Still no.

*Reveals: whether right and wrong travel with you or depend on where you are.*

### B55 The Bent Rule
Scene: Your 10-year-old wins a board game against their friends by quietly bending a rule. No one noticed. They're thrilled.
**Do you say something?**
- A: Yes, and they give the win back.
- B: No. It's a game, and they're happy.

*Reveals: whether small rules matter for their own sake — and what you think kids need to learn early.*

### B32 The Call-Up
Scene: Your country goes to war. You believe this war is wrong. You're called up to serve.
**What do you do?**
- A: I serve. My country asked.
- B: I refuse and accept the consequences, even prison.
- C: I leave the country for good.

Follow-up (if B or C): Now it's a different war. Your country has been invaded. Would you fight?
- A: Yes. Defending my home is different.
- B: No. I won't fight in any war.

*Reveals: conscience versus duty to your country — and whether you reject war itself or only wrong wars.*

## 8. Justice, punishment, and forgiveness

### B33 The Man Who Changed
Scene: At 19, a man badly hurt someone in a robbery and was never caught. He's now 45: a teacher, a father, a volunteer. New evidence surfaces.
**Should he go to prison?**
- A: Yes. Justice doesn't expire.
- B: No. He's not that person anymore.

Follow-up (if B): The person he hurt still lives with daily pain and wants him prosecuted.
- A: Then yes, prosecute.
- B: I understand, but no.

*Reveals: what punishment is for — paying back, protecting others, or changing people — and whether a person can outgrow a crime.*

### B34 The Remorse Pill
Scene: A pill makes anyone who takes it deeply sorry for their crime and unable to ever do it again. A person convicted of assault has taken it.
**Should they still serve their prison sentence?**
- A: Yes. They still deserve it.
- B: No. There's nothing left for prison to do.

Follow-up (if B): If they walk free, others may think crime is cheap.
- A: Then they should serve.
- B: That's not a reason to punish this person.

*Reveals: whether you believe in punishment for its own sake — or only as a warning to others.*

### B35 The True Story
Scene: Someone seriously hurt a person you love. Police looked into it, and nothing came of it. You have true, damaging information about them that would wreck their reputation if you posted it anonymously.
**Do you post it?**
- A: Yes. They earned it.
- B: No. That's not my role.

Follow-up (if A): It will also hurt their teenage kids, who did nothing.
- A: Still yes.
- B: Then no.

*Reveals: justice versus revenge — and whether you'd step outside the system when it fails.*

### B36 What Forgiveness Is Owed
Scene: Someone close to you betrayed you badly. They've apologized sincerely, and over a year they've really changed.
**Do you owe them forgiveness?**
- A: Yes. They've done what they can.
- B: No. Forgiveness is a gift, never a debt.

Follow-up (everyone): They ask to be as close as you were before.
- A: Yes. If I forgive, I forgive.
- B: No. Forgiving isn't the same as trusting again.

*Reveals: whether forgiveness is earned or given — and whether it's the same thing as trust.*

## 10. Everyday courage and self-respect

## 11. The inner life: envy, limits, and what makes a life good
