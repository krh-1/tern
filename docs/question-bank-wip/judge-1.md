# Judge 1 — Verdicts on draft-v1 (N01–N62)

Bar applied: every question must split thoughtful people, have no safe or face-saving answer, read in one pass, and add something that no existing Q or D and no other N already covers. Scoring and axes were ignored.

## A) Verdicts

### Theme 1 — Honesty

**N01 The Invisible Year — REFRAME.** The follow-up is excellent: it asks whether you think other people are like you. The trunk has face-saving pressure, because "C: Yes, quite a lot" sounds like confessing. I rewrote the answers so that changing your behaviour sounds normal, not guilty.

### N01 The Invisible Year
Scene: For one year, nothing you do can ever be traced back to you. No cameras, no witnesses, no consequences.
Question: Would you live any differently?
- A: No. Nothing would change.
- B: I'd bend a few small rules.
- C: I'd do some things I've always wanted to but never dared.
Follow-up (all): Now everyone gets the same year. By the end, is the world better or worse?
- A: Better. Most people are decent when no one's watching.
- B: Worse. Most people are decent because someone is watching.
- C: About the same.

**N02 The Deathbed Question — REFRAME (small).** A strong question with a real split. In the follow-up, answer B ("He can't have meant this") sounds like the person is making excuses. I reworded it so it states a value instead.

### N02 The Deathbed Question
Scene: Your grandfather has hours to live. He asks if the family business he spent his life building is doing well. It went bankrupt last year.
Question: What do you tell him?
- A: The truth, as gently as I can.
- B: That it's doing well.
- C: I change the subject so I don't have to lie.
Follow-up (if B or C): Years ago he told you, "Whatever happens, never lie to me. Not even at the end." Does that change your answer?
- A: Yes. I tell him the truth.
- B: No. Right now, his peace matters more than that promise.

**N03 The Reference Call — KEEP.** Honesty toward people you will never meet, weighed against kindness to the person you know. The follow-up raises the stakes in a way that shows why people answered as they did. Answer C, the policy dodge, is itself revealing.

**N04 The Company Line — REFRAME.** As written, "something vague but true" is an easy escape that most people will take. Having a customer ask directly takes that escape away.

### N04 The Company Line
Scene: Your manager tells you to blame a late order on a "supplier issue." The real reason is that your company put bigger clients first. A customer asks you directly: "Is it really the supplier?"
Question: What do you say?
- A: "Yes, it's the supplier." It's the company's line, not my lie.
- B: "I can't share the details."
- C: "No. We put bigger orders first."
Follow-up (if A): The customer is a small bakery that may lose a wedding order because of the delay.
- A: I still give the company line.
- B: I tell them the truth.

**N05 The Burned Manuscript — KEEP.** This is the Kafka dilemma: what a dead friend wanted against the value of what they made. It is distinct from D1 because here the person is dead and the loss is to the world. N60 is its sibling (see clusters).

### Theme 2 — Family and loyalty

**N06 The 2 A.M. Call — KEEP.** One of the strongest in the set. The follow-up, where an innocent stranger is arrested, is a real turn in context. It is close to D11 and Q3 but goes further: an active lie to police, and then a third party pays the price.

**N07 The Promise About the Home — KEEP.** This is D1 made concrete and more painful. The follow-up ("the person I promised isn't really there") shows the reasoning behind the answer. I recommend it replace D1 (see section D).

**N08 The Draft — REFRAME.** The follow-up fires for everyone, but for people who chose "I serve" it means nothing. I limited it to the others. I also dropped "leave the country" from the follow-up so the choice is fight or not.

### N08 The Draft
Scene: Your country goes to war. You believe this war is wrong. You're called up to serve.
Question: What do you do?
- A: I serve. My country asked.
- B: I refuse and accept the consequences, even prison.
- C: I serve, but in a role where I won't have to fight.
- D: I leave the country.
Follow-up (if B, C, or D): Now it's a different war. Your country has been invaded. Would you fight?
- A: Yes. Defending my home is different.
- B: No. I won't fight in any war.

**N09 The Cousin or the Stranger — CUT.** It repeats Q4 and D2 (local help versus effective help), and N15 covers "near versus far" more sharply.

**N10 The Dog or the Stranger — KEEP.** Short and uncomfortable, and surveys show a genuine split. Answering "my dog" is a revealing confession rather than a trap, because the people who pick it can explain why.

### Theme 3 — Luck and fairness

**N11 Two Drivers — KEEP.** Moral luck (whether a person is to blame for what they could not control), in plain words. The follow-up about punishment separates judging the person from what the law should do.

**N12 Two Worlds — REFRAME.** The follow-up only makes sense for people who chose World A; for anyone who chose B, "Yes, B" is confusing. I also sharpened the numbers.

### N12 Two Worlds
Scene: You'll be born into one of two worlds, as a random person. In World A, everyone has a decent, secure life, but no one is rich. In World B, most people live much better than in World A, but 1 in 10 people are poor.
Question: Which world do you choose?
- A: World A.
- B: World B.
Follow-up (if A): Now you know for sure you'll be born into the top half of World B. Do you switch?
- A: Yes, B.
- B: No, still A.

**N13 The Proud Thing — KEEP.** It turns the question of who deserves what onto the user's own life, which none of the existing questions do. Nobody is judged whichever answer they give.

**N14 The Lifeboat — REFRAME.** A good question about fair procedure. It gains a sharp follow-up: what if the lottery picks you? That tests whether people keep to a procedure when it costs them.

### N14 The Lifeboat
Scene: A lifeboat holds 8 people, but 9 are on it. If no one leaves, it sinks and everyone dies. You're one of the 9.
Question: How should it be decided who goes?
- A: Draw lots. Everyone takes the same chance.
- B: Ask for a volunteer.
- C: The person least likely to survive anyway.
- D: I go.
Follow-up (if A): The lots are drawn. It's you.
- A: I go. That was the deal.
- B: I argue for another way.

### Theme 4 — Harm and sacrifice

**N15 The Pond and the Postcard — REFRAME.** The original mixes what you believe with what you do in a single answer. "I do give" also sounds self-congratulatory, and D sounds cold. I split belief (trunk) from behaviour (follow-up) and wrote the follow-up so it does not accuse anyone.

### N15 The Pond and the Postcard
Scene: A small child is drowning in a pond in front of you. Saving them will ruin your $800 phone. Meanwhile, $800 given to a proven charity would very likely save a child's life far away.
Question: Is walking past the pond worse than not giving the $800?
- A: Yes, much worse. Being right there makes it mine.
- B: A little worse. Distance matters some.
- C: No. A child is a child.
Follow-up (if C): Most people don't live as if that's true. Do you?
- A: Mostly, yes.
- B: Not really, and it bothers me.
- C: Not really, and I'm at peace with that.

**N16 The Kidney — KEEP.** The trunk has an honest split, and "only for someone I know" is a respectable answer. The follow-up (the recipient is a released murderer) asks whether saving a life should depend on whether that person deserves it.

**N17 The Monkeys — CUT.** "Only if there's truly no other way" is a dodge that most people will choose. With three questions on animals, this is the weakest; N10 and N18 cover where people draw the line on which beings count.

**N18 The Slaughterhouse Test — KEEP.** It asks whether your moral lines hold up when you are close to the act. The follow-up about paying someone else to do it is a question nobody else in the set asks.

**N19 The Weapons Job — KEEP.** This is the classic "keep your own hands clean or make the outcome less bad" case. Two answers, a real split. It stands in for the whole group of "dirty hands" questions.

**N20 The Mayor's Deal — CUT.** It overlaps N19 (dirty hands) and N23 (bribery). Answer C, "No, and I report him," is the obvious answer that makes the answerer look good.

### Theme 5 — Strangers and future people

**N21 The Hundred-Year Button — REFRAME (small).** "10% harder" is vague, so I made the cost concrete. The follow-up (would you sacrifice alone, or only if everyone does?) is excellent.

### N21 The Hundred-Year Button
Scene: Pressing a button would give you 10% less money and comfort for the rest of your life. In 200 years, a million people you'll never meet would live noticeably better.
Question: Do you press it?
- A: Yes.
- B: No.
Follow-up (all): Now everyone alive today gets the same deal. It happens only if most people vote yes. How do you vote?
- A: Yes.
- B: No.

**N22 The Happy City — REFRAME.** The trunk is strong (it is Le Guin's Omelas). The follow-up ("your comfort rests on suffering far away") preaches, drifts into politics, and repeats N15. I cut the follow-up, tightened the scene, and added a follow-up that shows why people answered as they did.

### N22 The Happy City
Scene: You live in a city where everyone is healthy and happy. The city's good fortune depends on one child kept suffering alone in a basement. If the child is freed, the good fortune ends for everyone.
Question: What do you do?
- A: Stay. Leaving wouldn't help the child.
- B: Walk away. I can't live on that.
- C: Free the child, whatever it costs.
Follow-up (if A): It's not one child. It's a hundred.
- A: I'd still stay.
- B: Then I'd leave or act.

**N23 The Bribe Abroad — REFRAME.** "When in Rome" is an idiom that non-native English speakers may not know. "Pay, then report it later" is a strange hybrid. The follow-up adds a cost to refusing.

### N23 The Bribe Abroad
Scene: You're living in another country and need a permit you're entitled to. Everyone knows the official expects a small cash "fee." Locals pay it without thinking twice.
Question: Do you pay?
- A: Yes. That's how it works here.
- B: No, even if it takes months longer.
Follow-up (if B): Without the permit, you can't start the job you moved there for.
- A: Then I pay.
- B: Still no.

### Theme 6 — Rules and authority

**N24 The Red Light at 3 A.M. — KEEP.** Light, quick and revealing. The child-watching follow-up asks whether you follow rules for their own sake or to model them for others.

**N25 The Jury — KEEP.** Jury nullification (acquitting someone despite the law) in plain words. The gambling-debt follow-up isolates how much sympathy for the defendant is driving the verdict. It is distinct from Q9, which is about protest.

**N26 The Camera City — REFRAME.** "Only if footage is deleted after a month" is a middle answer that lets people escape the choice. Build the deletion into the scene, force a yes or no, and add a follow-up about who holds the power.

### N26 The Camera City
Scene: Your city can put cameras on every public street, recording all the time. Violent crime would likely drop by half. Footage is deleted after 30 days.
Question: Do you support it?
- A: Yes.
- B: No.
Follow-up (if A): A new mayor you don't trust will control the system.
- A: Still yes.
- B: Then no.

### Theme 7 — Justice and forgiveness

**N27 The Man Who Changed — KEEP.** What is punishment for, and is the man of 45 the same person as the young man of 19? The victim's follow-up shows what the answer depends on.

**N28 The Remorse Pill — REFRAME.** Its follow-up ("the victim needs to see them pay") repeated N27's. I replaced it with a deterrence follow-up (punishing one person to warn others) so the two questions test different reasons for punishment.

### N28 The Remorse Pill
Scene: A pill makes anyone who takes it deeply sorry for their crime and unable to ever do it again. A person convicted of assault has taken it.
Question: Should they still serve their prison sentence?
- A: Yes. They still deserve it.
- B: No. There's nothing left for prison to do.
- C: A shorter sentence.
Follow-up (if B or C): If they walk free, others may think crime is cheap.
- A: Then they should serve.
- B: That's not a reason to punish this person.

**N29 The True Story — KEEP.** Justice or revenge when the system fails you. The follow-up (the target's teenage kids will be hurt) is a real shift in context.

**N30 What Forgiveness Is Owed — REFRAME.** Answer C does not answer the question asked, and it is the obvious safe pick. Split it: forgiveness in the trunk, trust in the follow-up.

### N30 What Forgiveness Is Owed
Scene: Someone close to you betrayed you badly. They've apologized sincerely, and over a year they've really changed.
Question: Do you owe them forgiveness?
- A: Yes. They've done what they can.
- B: No. Forgiveness is a gift, never a debt.
Follow-up (all): They ask to be as close as you were before.
- A: Yes. If I forgive, I forgive.
- B: No. Forgiving isn't the same as trusting again.

**N31 The Old Posts — CUT.** It stands in for "cancel culture," which splits people along political lines. It also overlaps N27, where people change over time.

### Theme 10 — Complicity and courage

**N41 The Dinner Joke — REFRAME (small).** "Laugh along" makes the answerer look bad, so honest people will avoid it. Soften it to a recognisable, human choice. Add a follow-up where the absent group becomes personal.

### N41 The Dinner Joke
Scene: At a family dinner, a relative makes a cruel joke about a group of people. No one from that group is there. Most of the table laughs.
Question: What do you do?

- B: Talk to them privately later.
- C: Don't laugh, but let it go.
- D: Smile along to keep the peace.
Follow-up (if C or D): Your close friend belongs to that group.
- A: Then I'd say something.
- B: I'd still let it go.

## C) Coverage gaps, with new proposals

What is still missing after the cuts:
- **You as the one who did wrong.** Almost every question has you judging someone else.
- **Envy.**
- **Merit versus equal shares** among people you know.
- **Trust and privacy** with a partner.
- **Self-respect**: being right versus keeping the peace.
- **Presumption of innocence**, done without the heaviness of N43.
- **Mercy versus consistency.**
- **Duty to yourself**: when caring for others costs your own life.

### G01 The Mistake No One Saw
Scene: Years ago, you made a mistake that cost a coworker a promotion. They never found out it was you. You run into them now, and they're doing fine.
Question: Do you tell them?
- A: Yes, and apologize.
- B: No. It would only hurt them now.
- C: No. I'd rather make it up to them some other way.
Follow-up (if B or C): They mention that losing that promotion still makes them doubt themselves.
- A: Then I tell them.
- B: Still no.
Reveals: whether an apology is for the person wronged or for yourself; accountability versus peace.

### G02 The Friend's Big Break
Scene: Your close friend suddenly gets the exact success you've wanted for years. They call you first to share the news. Most people feel a mix of things in moments like this.
Question: Honestly, what's strongest?
- A: Happiness for them.
- B: Happiness, with a sting.
- C: Mostly the sting.
Follow-up (all): You learn they got it mostly through luck, not more talent than you.
- A: That makes it easier.
- B: That makes it harder.
Reveals: how you relate to envy, and whether deserving it changes how you feel about someone else's good fortune.

### G03 The Shared Prize
Scene: You and three coworkers win a $20,000 bonus for a project. You did about half the work. The others split the rest evenly.
Question: How should the money be split?
- A: Equally, $5,000 each.
- B: By work. I get about half.
- C: Mostly equal, a little more to me.
Follow-up (if B or C): The others assume an equal split. You'd have to ask for more out loud.
- A: I ask.
- B: I let it go.
Reveals: equal shares versus earned shares, and whether you would stand up for your own claim.

### G04 The Unlocked Phone
Scene: Your partner leaves their phone unlocked on the table and steps into the shower. Lately you've had a vague feeling something is off.
Question: Do you look?
- A: No. That's their private space.
- B: Yes, just a quick look.
- C: No, but I ask them about it tonight.
Follow-up (if A or C): A friend says they saw your partner on what looked like a date.
- A: Now I look.
- B: Still no. I ask them directly.
Reveals: trust and privacy, weighed against protecting yourself.

### G05 The Apology They Want
Scene: You're in a dispute with a neighbor, and you're sure you're right. They say they'll drop it if you apologize. That would make everything easy.
Question: Do you apologize?
- A: Yes. Peace is worth more than being right.
- B: No. I won't say sorry for something I didn't do.
- C: I say I'm sorry it happened, not that I was wrong.
Follow-up (if A or C): Later, they tell other neighbors you admitted you were wrong.
- A: I let it go.
- B: I set the record straight.
Reveals: self-respect, and truth versus keeping the peace.

### G06 The Rumor at Work
Scene: A coworker you like is rumored to have lied on a report to cover a mistake. There's no proof, and they deny it. Your team is quietly avoiding them.
Question: What do you do?
- A: Treat them the same as always.
- B: Keep some distance until it's cleared up.
- C: Ask them about it directly.
Follow-up (if A or C): Being seen with them could hurt your standing on the team.
- A: I still treat them the same.
- B: Then I keep some distance.
Reveals: presumption of innocence (treating someone as innocent until proven guilty) and the social courage it takes, without N43's heavy subject.

### G07 The Late Paper
Scene: You're a teacher with a firm deadline rule. A student who had a hard week asks for an extension. Others handed theirs in on time, some while struggling too.
Question: Do you give the extension?
- A: Yes. Their week was hard.
- B: No. The rule is the same for everyone.
- C: Yes, and I offer the same to the whole class.
Follow-up (if A): You learn several other students had hard weeks too but didn't ask.
- A: I offer it to them as well.
- B: Only the one who asked gets it.
Reveals: mercy versus consistency, and whether people who ask should get more.

### G08 The Nightly Call
Scene: A friend going through a hard time calls you every night for an hour. It's wearing you down, and your own life is slipping.
Question: What do you do?
- A: Keep taking every call.
- B: Tell them honestly I need to pull back.
- C: Slowly answer less, without saying why.
Follow-up (if B or C): They say you're the only person they can talk to.
- A: Then I keep going.
- B: I hold the line, and help them find others.
Reveals: what you owe others versus what you owe yourself, and whether you are direct about limits or avoid saying them.

## D) Existing set: questions that fail the rubric

**The pattern across the set.** Most questions offer a compromise "third way" (Q4-C, Q5-C, Q6-C, Q7-C, Q9-C, Q11-C, D1-C, D3-C, D5-C, D6-C, D8-C, D9-C, D10-C, D12-C). These let the user escape the tension and will dominate the answers. **Recommendation:** remove each compromise option, or attach a cost to it that the user has to accept.

- **Q2 The Wallet.** A is the obvious answer, and almost nobody will admit to C. Fix: drop C, and set up the scene so that returning the cash has a small cost (for example, the owner lives an hour away).
- **Q3 follow-up.** Answer C ("Same ultimatum, but with more empathy") only makes sense for people who chose trunk C, and it is not parallel with the other answers. Fix: A "No, I do what I planned," B "Yes, I stay out of it."
- **Q6 follow-up.** It mostly restates the trunk. Fix: replace it with a new fact, for example that the colleague would do the same for you, or that the colleague once took credit for your work.
- **Q7 The White Lie.** C ("honest, carefully and gently") is the obviously correct answer. Fix: remove C and add the assumption "There's no gentle version. They ask: 'Will this work, yes or no?'"
- **Q9 The Protest.** C ("exhaust legal options first") is a dodge that contradicts the question's own premise that legal routes are slow. Fix: assume legal routes have already failed and remove C.
- **Q10 follow-up.** B ("move the child to safety") is obvious, and C does not fit with the other answers. Fix: have the follow-up take away the safe option, for example "The staff are not coming."
- **Q11 The Autonomous Car.** C ("defer to regulators") dodges a question that begins "if you set the policy." The trunk also repeats Q1. Fix: cut C, and consider a follow-up where the car must choose between its own passenger and pedestrians, which is new ground.
- **D1 The Promise Under Pressure.** It is abstract ("a clearly worse outcome for everyone"). Fix: replace it with N07.
- **D4 The Stranded Stranger.** C ("call a service") is the obvious answer, and B sounds callous. Fix: assume no service can be reached, so the choice is to stop or drive on.
- **D11 The Silent Witness.** B ("the truth, framed favorably") will be picked by almost everyone. Fix: set up the scene so that any softening would amount to a lie by omission.
- **D12 The Efficiency Argument.** It uses jargon ("high-performing initiative," "total output"), and C is the modal answer. Fix: rewrite it about real people, for example "Give most of the money to your best employee's project, or split it evenly across the team?", and drop C.
