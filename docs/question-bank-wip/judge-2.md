## A) Verdicts

### 1. Truth and what we owe it

**B01 The Invisible Year — FIX.** The trunk is fine. In the follow-up, "C: About the same" is the fence-sit that absorbs anyone who doesn't want to commit to a view of humanity. It's the whole point of the follow-up, so force the call.
Follow-up (everyone): Now everyone gets the same year. By the end, is the world better or worse?
- A: Better. Most people are decent when no one's watching.
- B: Worse. Most people are decent because someone is watching.

**B02 The Deathbed Question — PASS.** A real split, a revealing dodge option (C), and the follow-up turns his own words against comfort. One of the best in the bank.

**B03 The Reference Call — FIX.** People who pick C ("I don't give references") never meet the follow-up, so the policy dodge gets a free pass. Fire the follow-up for C too, and make its wording work for both groups.
Follow-up (if B or C): The job is overnight care for elderly patients. A missed shift means someone goes without help.
- A: Then I tell them about the reliability problem.
- B: I stick with what I said.

**B04 The Company Line — FIX.** Judge 1 said the direct question takes away the escape, but B ("I can't share the details") is still there, and it's what most people will pick. Take it out, and put a real cost on telling the truth so honesty isn't free virtue-signaling.
Scene: Your manager tells you to blame a late order on a "supplier issue." The real reason is that your company put bigger clients first. With your manager standing right there, a customer asks you: "Is it really the supplier?"
- A: "Yes, it's the supplier." It's the company's line, not my lie.
- B: "No. We put bigger orders first."

**B05 The Mistake No One Saw — FIX.** "A mistake that cost a coworker a promotion" is too vague to picture. C ("make it up some other way") is a fog answer: make it up how? Make the scene concrete and give C a specific action.
Scene: Years ago, you made a mistake on a shared project, and your coworker took the blame. It cost them a promotion. They never found out, and today they're doing fine.
- C: No. But I quietly help their career now.

### 2. Promises, and people who are gone

**B06 The Unread Manuscript — FIX.** C ("keep it private, don't delete") looks like a gentle middle, but it breaks the promise. Most people won't notice that, so C becomes the easy way out. Say plainly what C costs, and add a follow-up that makes C-choosers face where it ends.
- C: I break the promise quietly: I keep it and show no one.

Follow-up (if C): You won't live forever. What happens to the file when you die?
- A: I delete it before then.
- B: I leave it for someone to find.

**B07 The Promise About the Home — FIX.** The follow-up fires for everyone, but people who picked B already broke the promise. For them, "a promise is a promise" makes no sense. Fire it only for A.
Follow-up (if A): They no longer recognize you. They're calm with anyone who is kind to them. Does that change things?
- A: Yes. I move them. The person I promised isn't really there anymore.
- B: No. It's still them, and a promise is a promise.

**B08 The Family Secret — PASS.** Short, a real split, no escape option. "Something terrible" is vague, but that's safer than naming a specific war.

### 3. Loyalty and family

**B09 The 2 A.M. Call — FIX.** The scene is four sentences. Trim it to three with no change in meaning.
Scene: At 2 a.m., your sibling calls: they hit a cyclist and drove off in a panic. The cyclist will recover, and the police have no leads. Your sibling asks you to say they were with you tonight.

**B10 What We Owe Our Parents — FIX.** It's abstract, with no scene to picture, and B ("love and care, but not obedience") is the answer almost everyone gives. C sounds cold. Replace it with a concrete choice where gratitude has to pay out. The cultural split on this is huge and real.
Scene: Your parents gave up a lot to raise you. They strongly oppose the person you want to marry. There's nothing wrong with this person; your parents just don't trust them.
**Do you marry them anyway?**
- A: No. After everything, I owe my parents that.
- B: Yes. What they gave me doesn't buy a veto.

**B11 The Bully's Son — FIX.** "Equally strong" plus "flip a coin" hands everyone a clean, principled answer, and the honest lean gets lost. Ask for the lean. Keep the coin for people who really have none.
**Honestly, who do you lean toward?**
- A: The other candidate.
- B: The son.
- C: No lean. I'd flip a coin.

*Note: this mirrors D5 (hiring your mentor's kid). Don't show both in one session.*

### 4. Luck, deserving, and fairness

**B12 Two Drivers — PASS.** Moral luck in plain words. Thoughtful people really do split on it, and the follow-up separates blame from punishment.

**B13 Two Worlds — FIX.** "Poor" means different things in different countries, so readers picture different worlds. Make it concrete.
Scene: You'll be born into one of two worlds, as a random person. In World A, everyone has a decent, secure life, but no one is rich. In World B, most people live much better than in World A, but 1 in 10 struggle to afford food and a home.

**B14 The Proud Thing — FIX.** The follow-up ("does that mean you deserve it less?") makes no sense for someone who just said "mostly me." Fire it only where luck was admitted.
Follow-up (if B or C): Does that mean you deserve it less?

**B15 The Lifeboat — FIX.** B ("ask for a volunteer") hands the choice to someone else. Close that gap with a follow-up for B.
Follow-up (if B): No one volunteers. Now what?
- A: Draw lots.
- B: The person least likely to survive.
- C: I go.

**B16 The Shared Bonus — PASS.** The trunk's C is a soft middle, but the follow-up (would you actually ask out loud?) catches it well.

**B17 The Late Paper — FIX.** C ("yes, and offer it to the whole class") lets everyone win, and it will be the top pick. Remove it. The follow-up for A already tests fairness to people who didn't ask.
- A: Yes. Their week was hard.
- B: No. The rule is the same for everyone.

### 5. Sacrifice and harm

**B18 The Pond and the Faraway Child — PASS.** Peter Singer's pond argument (walking past a drowning child vs. not giving to save a child far away) at its cleanest, and the follow-up to C is one of the most honest in the bank. It overlaps in theme with Q4 and D2 (local help vs. effective help), but here it's asked as duty, not as a choice of charity.

**B19 The Kidney — FIX.** "Yes" costs nothing in a hypothetical. Real donation to strangers is rare, so A will be inflated. Name the real cost so people answer as themselves.
Scene: You learn you're a match for a stranger who will die without a kidney. The surgery is safe, but it means six weeks of painful recovery and a small lifelong risk.

**B20 The Weapons Job — PASS.** Bernard Williams' "George" case (take a job you oppose so someone worse doesn't get it), simply put. Two options, no escape, a real split.

**B21 The Million-Dollar Button — FIX.** People who say No never get tested on *why*. Is it the harm, or the principle of taking? Add a follow-up for them.
Follow-up (if B): The stranger is a billionaire who would never notice.
- A: Then yes.
- B: Still no.

**B22 The 200-Year Button — PASS.** The "alone vs. only if everyone does" follow-up is excellent. (Consider retitling it "The 200-Year Deal" so it doesn't sit right after another button.)

**B23 The Happy City — PASS.** Omelas (a city whose happiness depends on one suffering child). A is defensible because its reason is built in, so no one is shamed out of it, and the "hundred" follow-up finds your number.

### 6. Who counts: animals and machines

**B24 The Dog or the Stranger — PASS.** Two lines, a real split, and one of the most talked-about answers in the bank. Social pressure pushes toward "stranger," but the dog-savers who admit it are exactly the signal.

**B25 The Slaughterhouse Test — PASS.** D covers people who don't eat meat, and the follow-up gets at hypocrisy without preaching.

**B26 The Pleading Robot — PASS.** B ("yes, but it bothers me") will lead, but the gap between A and B is itself revealing. Keep it for depth, not core.

**B27 The AI Companion — FIX.** C ("only if they ask") is the escape, and most people will take it. Remove it, and add the follow-up where the ask actually happens. That's where the lie gets real.
- A: Yes. They should know what it is.
- B: No. Their happiness is real.

Follow-up (if B): One day they ask you: "Do you think it really loves me?"
- A: I tell them what I really think.
- B: I tell them yes.

### 7. Rules, law, and authority

**B28 The Red Light at 3 A.M. — PASS.** Light, quick, and the child-watching follow-up is a clever turn.

**B29 The Jury — FIX.** The follow-up fires for everyone, but anyone who voted guilty for the baby formula will vote guilty again, so the answer carries no information. Fire it only for B.
Follow-up (if B): Same law, same evidence, but they stole to pay off a gambling debt.

**B30 The Permit Fee — PASS.** The follow-up puts a price on the principled "no." That's exactly right.

**B31 The Camera City — CUT.** This is a policy poll, not a personal dilemma. The answer mostly tracks your politics and how much you trust government. The follow-up ("a mayor you don't trust") nearly always flips people, so it measures trust, not values. B01 and B44 already cover privacy and being watched.

**B32 The Call-Up — PASS.** Four realistic options, and the follow-up separates "wrong war" from "any war." The premise ("you believe this war is wrong") keeps it from being a political proxy.

### 8. Justice, punishment, and forgiveness

**B33 The Man Who Changed — FIX.** The follow-up fires for everyone, but people who already said "yes, prison" learn nothing from it. Limit it to B and C.
Follow-up (if B or C): The person he hurt still lives with daily pain and wants him prosecuted.
- A: Then yes, prosecute.
- B: I understand, but no.

**B34 The Remorse Pill — PASS.** It's the pure form of B33's question (is punishment payback or protection?). Keep both, but never in the same session.

**B35 The True Story — FIX.** C ("take it to someone official") is the escape, and it contradicts "got away with it." Say the system already failed, and drop C.
Scene: Someone seriously hurt a person you love. Police looked into it, and nothing came of it. You have true, damaging information about them that would wreck their reputation if you posted it anonymously.
- A: Yes. They earned it.
- B: No. That's not my role.

**B36 What Forgiveness Is Owed — PASS.** Clean, and it splits people. The trust follow-up pulls apart two things people usually mash together.

## B) Set-level balance

- **Water and drowning repeat.** B15 (lifeboat), B18 (pond), and B24 (dog/stranger) are all "someone is drowning." Never show more than one per session. Consider moving B24 to a fire.
- **Death and dying are heavy.** B02, B06, B07, B08, B12, B15, B18, B19, B23, B24, and B48 involve death, dying, or severe harm. That's about 20% of the bank. A run could easily feel grim, so cap it at two heavy scenes in a row.
- **Workplace is overrepresented.** B03, B04, B05, B11, B16, B17, B20, B41, and B42 (9), plus Q6, Q8, D3, D5, D6, D9, and D12 in the existing set. Many users are students, retired, or not in office jobs. Trim or reframe two or three (B16 works as a group project, B42 as friends).
- **Thought experiments vs. real life.** About 10 are sci-fi or philosophy-class setups (B01, B13, B21, B22, B23, B26, B34, B39, B47, B48). That's a good dose, but they should be spread out so the app doesn't feel like a seminar.
- **Underrepresented:** romantic partners (only B44), friends as peers (B45 and B46 only), money in everyday life (splitting bills, borrowing, debt), online life (posting, screenshots, anonymity), and being a parent (only B28 and B39). Also missing: faith or meaning, and neighbors or strangers in public (only Q10).
- **Light vs. heavy.** Truly light: B28, B45, B50–B53, maybe B14 and B16. That's about 8 of 53. The quick reads help, but the "max two per session" rule leaves few breathers. Add 3–4 light scenes (not quick reads).
- **Option counts are healthy.** About 17 two-option, about 26 three-option, about 8 four-option. After my fixes (B04, B10, B17, B27, B35, B53 go to two), two-option rises to about 23. That's fine for pace, and it cuts escape options.
- **Recurring escape pattern.** Judge 1 caught the compromise options in Q/D, but the same pattern survived in the bank: "I can't share" (B04), "only if they ask" (B27), "go official" (B35), "offer to everyone" (B17), "a few things" (B53). All are fixed above. Watch for this pattern in any new drafts.
- **Follow-up triggers.** Five follow-ups fired for people they couldn't reveal anything about (B03, B07, B14, B29, B33). Rule of thumb: a follow-up only fires for people who could plausibly change their answer.
- **Pairs to keep apart in one session:** B33/B34 (punishment), B21/B22 (buttons), B47/B49 (feeling vs. meaning), B07/B37 (past self vs. present self), B11/D5 (hiring bias), B26/B27 (machines), B02/B27 (truth vs. comfort with an elder).

## C) Top 12 — candidates for the fixed core set

My recommendation: these are the questions I'd most want every user to have seen, because they split people cleanly and give strangers something to compare.

1. **B01 The Invisible Year:** the best opener. It's light, it's about you, and the follow-up makes you judge everyone else.
2. **B02 The Deathbed Question:** everyone has an elder, everyone has an answer, and the follow-up flips people.
3. **B06 The Unread Manuscript:** a famous dilemma (Kafka's unpublished work) that people love arguing about, and it's distinct from every Q/D.
4. **B09 The 2 A.M. Call:** the strongest loyalty question, and its follow-up (an innocent stranger arrested) is the best turn in the bank.
5. **B12 Two Drivers:** moral luck (being judged for what happened vs. what you chose). Easy to grasp, and friends argue about it.
6. **B18 The Pond and the Faraway Child:** the gap between belief and action, asked without preaching.
7. **B24 The Dog or the Stranger:** two lines, instantly shareable, and it gets people talking.
8. **B36 What Forgiveness Is Owed:** universal, personal, and it separates forgiving from trusting again.
9. **B37 The Bank Card:** a new question (which self decides?) with no escape and a near 50/50 split.
10. **B40 The Dinner Joke (fixed):** everyday courage. Everyone has sat at this table.
11. **B45 The Friend's Big Break (fixed):** the only question that asks for an honest feeling, not a choice. It breaks the rhythm of the set.
12. **B47 The Perfect Life Machine:** the reverse follow-up (you're already plugged in) is the most "huh" moment in the bank.

Balance check on this 12: 4 heavy (B02, B09, B12, B18), 5 medium, 3 light (B01, B24, B45). No workplace questions, which offsets the workplace-heavy Q set. Two drowning scenes (B18, B24): keep them far apart in the order, or move B24 to a fire.
